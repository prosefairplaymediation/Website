# Side problem: getting your 3 observations

You've said the blocker on Supreme Court family certification is finding
mediators willing to let you observe. That's a real and common bottleneck — sitting
an observer costs the host mediator party consent, room logistics, and time, with
nothing in return. Worth solving, because the credential materially expands both

Approaches, roughly in order of hit rate:

1. **County court-connected mediation programs.** Highest-yield by far. These run
   high volumes of small-claims and family cases, often with volunteer mediators,
   and they're accustomed to hosting trainees because that's how they staff
   themselves. Call your circuit's **ADR / Court Mediation office** directly and
   ask specifically about observation slots for a certification candidate. Offer
   to volunteer afterward — that trade is the one that usually closes.

2. **The Florida DRC** (Dispute Resolution Center, Office of the State Courts
   Administrator). They administer certification, they know the requirement is a
   chokepoint, and they can often point you at circuits actively hosting
   observers. Ask them where candidates in your area are getting hours.

3. **Your training provider.** The organization that ran your 40-hour course has
   an alumni pipeline and a reputational interest in your completing. Many run
   observation-matching informally but only for people who ask. Go back to them.

4. **Local mediation associations and your county bar's ADR section.** Meetings
   are where you meet the people who can host you. Ask in person, not by email —
   cold email on this converts terribly.

5. **`r/Mediation`.** Note this reverses the advice in
   `../reddit/02-subreddit-map.md`, which puts that sub in Tier 3 — correctly, for
   *client reach*. For **peer networking it's the right room**: it's mostly
   practitioners, and "Florida candidate looking for observation hours, happy to
   travel" is exactly on-topic there. Ask openly; it costs nothing.

6. **Offer something back.** The ask converts far better with a trade attached —
   intake calls, scheduling, note-taking, or volunteering at the county program.
   You're asking for a real favor; make it cheaper to say yes.

Small framing point: ask for **one** observation, not three. "Can I sit in on a
session sometime?" is a much easier yes than "I need three," and hosts who let
you sit once usually let you sit again — and refer you to colleagues.
