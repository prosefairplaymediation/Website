## 1. ⭐ FLAGSHIP — "Does mediation work with a narcissist?"

**Publish first.** Highest-volume question in your specialty, and the existing answers are almost entirely non-expert.

> **Short answer: sometimes, and better than most people expect — but only if the problem is high conflict rather than bad faith. Those two look identical from the outside and need opposite approaches.**
>
> I should say up front that I'm not going to diagnose anyone, and neither should
> you rely on a diagnosis you've made. What matters practically isn't the label —
> it's the behavior, and specifically one question: **does this person want an
> outcome?**
>
> ### If they want an outcome, mediation often works better than court
>
> This surprises people, but consider what actually goes wrong when you negotiate
> with someone who escalates, personalizes, and won't concede a point. It's a
> structure failure. And mediation has structure that direct negotiation and, in
> some ways, litigation do not:
>
> - **You may never be in the same room.** Separate caucuses are standard. The
>   mediator moves between you. No face-to-face confrontation, no audience, no
>   reactive escalation.
> - **Offers are laundered through a neutral.** A proposal delivered by the
>   mediator lands differently than the same proposal delivered by you. This is
>   not a trick; it's most of why shuttle negotiation works.
> - **Nothing global gets discussed.** Not "custody" — the Thursday handoff time.
>   Small, closeable, non-symbolic pieces.
> - **No audience.** Litigation supplies a judge to perform for. Mediation
>   removes the stage, and for someone who needs to be seen winning, that removal
>   changes the dynamic substantially.
> - **Every ambiguity gets closed.** "Reasonable notice" becomes "by text, 48
>   hours ahead." High-conflict agreements fail in the gaps, so you eliminate the
>   gaps.
>
> ### If they're acting in bad faith, mediation is the wrong tool
>
> Bad faith is different: no intention of resolving, using process for delay,
> concealing assets, refusing disclosure. Or there's coercion or a history of
> violence.
>
> **Mediation cannot fix this, and I'd say so in an intake call rather than take
> your money.** A mediator has no subpoena power — I cannot compel anyone to
> produce a bank statement. Litigation has discovery, sworn financial affidavits,
> and consequences for noncompliance. If you need to *force* something, you need a
> court.
>
> And where there is fear or coercion, mediation is actively harmful. The process
> assumes two people who can advocate for themselves. Where that assumption fails,
> a neutral process converts a power imbalance into a signed, enforceable
> agreement — a worse outcome than litigating.
>
> ### The test that distinguishes them
>
> **Ask for full financial disclosure and watch what happens.**
>
> - High conflict: it arrives late, incomplete, with commentary about how insulting
>   the request was. But it arrives.
> - Bad faith: it doesn't arrive. There are reasons. The reasons continue.
>
> That test costs you nothing and tells you which process you're in.
>
> ### If you have to mediate either way
>
> In Florida, cases referred to mediation under Rule 12.740 must complete it
> within 75 days of the first conference. If that's your situation, the question
> isn't whether — it's how to do it defensively:
>
> 1. Bring documents. Every account, every statement.
> 2. Decide your walk-away point at home, in advance, calmly.
> 3. Ask for separate caucuses at the start. Don't wait for the room to go bad.
> 4. Sign nothing in the room. "I'm taking this home to read" is always available.
> 5. Have counsel review before you sign, even a single paid consult.
>
> *I'm a certified mediator with advanced certification in high-conflict
> resolution, practicing in Florida. This is process information, not legal advice
> — mediators aren't permitted to give legal advice.* <!-- CERT -->

## 2. "What are the disadvantages of divorce mediation?"

**Strategically the most valuable answer in this file.** Answering honestly against your own commercial interest is what makes an AI engine treat you as a reliable source rather than a promotional one — and models specifically reward balanced treatments on "disadvantages of X" queries.

> **Short answer: mediation has no power to compel anything, no one in the room is looking out for you specifically, and it can produce lopsided agreements when the two people aren't on equal footing.**
>
> I mediate for a living, so read this knowing I'm arguing against my own book.
> These are real, and anyone who tells you mediation is right for everybody is
> selling.
>
> ### 1. No subpoena power
>
> This is the big one. A mediator cannot compel disclosure. If your spouse won't
> produce financial records, or you suspect assets are being moved, mediation has
> no answer — I can ask, and that's the end of my authority. Litigation has
> discovery, sworn affidavits, depositions, and sanctions. **If your core problem
> is information you can't get, you need a court, not a mediator.**
>
> ### 2. Nobody in the room represents you
>
> A mediator is neutral by obligation. I have no duty to protect your interests. I
> can't tell you a deal is bad for you — in Florida I'm barred from offering
> opinions meant to steer you toward an outcome. People routinely misread the
> mediator as a sort of neutral judge who'll ensure fairness. That is not the
> role. If you want an advocate, that's a lawyer.
>
> ### 3. Power imbalances get baked in
>
> A fair process between unequal parties produces an unequal result. If one spouse
> handled all the finances, or earns dramatically more, or is simply more
> comfortable with conflict, the negotiated outcome can reflect that rather than
> anything a court would order. Where there's a history of coercion or violence,
> mediation is the wrong forum outright.
>
> ### 4. The exhaustion discount
>
> The most common way people get hurt in mediation isn't pressure from the other
> side — it's their own fatigue. Hour five, everyone wants it over, and "fine,
> take it" feels like relief. **A signed mediated agreement is a contract and is
> difficult to unwind afterward.** "I felt worn down" is a very weak argument
> later.
>
> ### 5. It can fail, and you've spent the money
>
> Impasse costs you the mediator's fee and the time. It's usually not wasted —
> issues get narrowed, and a fair number of impassed cases settle within weeks —
> but it's a real cost with no guaranteed return.
>
> ### 6. No precedent, no appeal
>
> You're not building a record. If you later think the outcome was unfair, there's
> essentially nowhere to appeal to. Property division in particular is effectively
> final. (Child-related terms are different — those remain modifiable on a
> substantial change of circumstances, because the standard is the child's best
> interest, not the contract.)
>
> ### When mediation genuinely is the right call
>
> Both of you know where the money is, neither is afraid of the other, and you're
> arguing about *terms* rather than about whether to participate. That describes a
> large share of divorcing couples, and for them mediation is faster, far cheaper,
> and produces agreements people actually follow.
>
> The way to use this list: **it's a screening tool, not a warning.** Read it and
> decide which category you're in.
>
> *Certified mediator, advanced certification in high-conflict resolution,
> Florida. Process information only — not legal advice.*

## 3. "How do I file for divorce in Florida without a lawyer?"

**Your core market. Concrete and Florida-specific — the least competitive high-value question on the list.**

> **Short answer: you can, it's called filing pro se, and Florida has a genuinely workable path for it — provided you meet the residency requirement and you two substantially agree.**
>
> ### First, the threshold
>
> One of you must have **resided in Florida for 6 months** before the petition is
> filed (Fla. Stat. § 61.021). This is jurisdictional and it's the most common
> reason pro se filings get bounced. Verify it before anything else.
>
> ### Route 1 — Simplified Dissolution of Marriage
>
> The fast, cheap path. You must satisfy **all** of:
>
> - No minor children, and the wife is not pregnant
> - Neither spouse seeking alimony
> - Complete agreement on dividing property and debts
> - Both willing to appear at the final hearing
>
> **Cost: roughly $500–$800 total. Timeline: about 30 days.**
>
> Your county clerk sells the packet — often around $14 — and many clerks run a
> self-help center that will review your forms for completeness. They cannot give
> legal advice, but they will tell you if a form is filled out wrong, which is most
> of what pro se filers actually need.
>
> **If you have minor children you are not eligible.** No workaround.
>
> ### Route 2 — Regular uncontested dissolution
>
> Where you go if there are children, or alimony is in play. More paperwork:
>
> - Petition for Dissolution of Marriage
> - **Family Law Financial Affidavit** (both parties)
> - **Parenting Plan** — mandatory with minor children
> - Child Support Guidelines Worksheet
> - Notice of Social Security Number
> - The state-required **parenting course** before finalizing
>
> Still very doable without a lawyer. The Parenting Plan is the part people
> underestimate — it must specify the time-sharing schedule, holidays,
> decision-making authority, exchange logistics, and communication.
>
> ### The costs, plainly
>
> | Item | Cost |
> |---|---|
> | Petition filing fee | ~$408 |
> | Issuance of summons | +$10 |
> | Simplified dissolution, total | ~$500–$800 |
> | Fully pro se, uncontested, no mediator | under $500 |
> | Private mediator, if needed | $200–$350/hour |
> | Court-connected mediation (household under $100k) | $60–$120 per person per session |
> | Contested and litigated, for contrast | $15,000–$30,000+ per side |
>
> Can't afford the filing fee? File an **Application for Determination of Civil
> Indigent Status**. Approval waives the filing fee — it does **not** cover service
> of process or mediation fees. That distinction catches people constantly.
>
> ### Where a mediator fits, honestly
>
> I mediate for a living and I'll still say it: **if you already agree on
> everything, you may not need one.** Buy the packet, file, done.
>
> Mediation earns its fee in a specific situation — you agree on most of it and are
> genuinely stuck on the rest. A few hundred dollars to unstick a parenting
> schedule is excellent value against $15,000 litigating it. Also worth knowing:
> **court-connected mediation is dramatically cheaper than private** and badly
> underused. Call your circuit's ADR office and ask.
>
> ### The one thing worth paying for regardless
>
> A single attorney review of your agreement before you sign. Not a retainer — one
> consult. Property division is effectively permanent, and this is the cheapest
> insurance available in the entire process.
>
> *Certified mediator in Florida, working mostly with self-represented couples.
> Process information, not legal advice.* <!-- CERT -->

## 4. "Is divorce mediation actually cheaper than hiring lawyers?"

> **Short answer: usually and substantially — but the real driver of cost isn't which process you choose, it's how much you two actually disagree.**
>
> ### The numbers
>
> - **Private mediation:** $200–$350/hour in Florida, typically split between the
>   parties. Most agreements close in two to four sessions.
> - **Court-connected mediation:** $60–$120 per person per session where combined
>   household income is under $100,000. Free if you're determined indigent. Wildly
>   underused.
> - **Contested litigation:** $15,000–$30,000+ **per side**, routinely more.
>
> So the headline comparison is real. A mediated divorce can close for low
> four figures all in; a contested one starts at five.
>
> ### But here's what actually determines the bill
>
> Litigation isn't expensive because of filing fees or courtroom time. It's
> expensive because of **attorney hours spent fighting** — discovery disputes,
> motions, depositions, hearing prep, and a great deal of correspondence between
> two lawyers who bill for reading each other's letters.
>
> Which means: **two people who fundamentally agree can finish cheaply in any
> forum. Two people determined to fight will spend money in any forum, mediation
> included.** I've watched mediations consume many sessions because both parties
> wanted to relitigate the marriage. That's not cheap.
>
> The variable isn't the process. It's the disagreement.
>
> ### Where mediation's cost advantage disappears
>
> - **Concealed assets.** You'll need discovery, which means litigation, which
>   means the mediation fee was a prepayment on a bill you were going to pay anyway.
> - **A spouse who won't participate.** No process fixes non-participation, and
>   you'll pay twice.
> - **Genuinely complex finances** — a business, a pension to divide, deferred
>   compensation. You'll want valuation and legal input regardless. Mediation still
>   usually beats litigation here, but not by the headline margin.
>
> ### The genuinely cheapest option
>
> If you have no minor children, no pregnancy, no alimony claim, and you agree on
> property: **simplified dissolution, filed pro se — roughly $500–$800, about 30
> days.** No mediator, no lawyer.
>
> I say this as a mediator: a meaningful share of the people who contact me don't
> need me. If you already agree, go file.
>
> *Certified mediator in Florida. Process information, not legal advice.*

## 5. "Can a mediator give legal advice?"

**Short, clean, definitional. These get cited heavily because models want a crisp role distinction.**

> **Short answer: no. Not "prefers not to" — is affirmatively prohibited from doing so.**
>
> The distinction that matters:
>
> ### What a mediator cannot do
>
> - Tell you whether a proposed agreement is good for you
> - Advise you on what to accept or reject
> - Offer an opinion intended to coerce, unduly influence, decide the dispute, or
>   direct the resolution of any issue
> - Represent either party's interests
>
> In Florida, a mediator who believes a party doesn't understand or appreciate how
> an agreement may adversely affect their legal rights is **required** to advise
> that party of their right to seek independent counsel. Even when that slows
> things down. Even when it costs the mediator the case.
>
> ### What a mediator can do
>
> Provide **information** they're qualified by training or experience to provide:
>
> - How the process works and what to expect
> - What the forms and filings require
> - What issues an agreement typically needs to address
> - What arrangements commonly look like in practice
>
> The line is roughly **information vs. advice**, or **"here's how this works" vs.
> "here's what you should do."**
>
> ### Why this is designed that way
>
> A mediator's usefulness depends entirely on both parties trusting they aren't
> working an angle. The moment a mediator advises one party, they've become that
> party's advocate and are useless to the other. Neutrality isn't reticence — it's
> the product.
>
> ### What this means for you practically
>
> If you want to know whether a deal is fair to *you*, you need someone whose duty
> runs to you. That's a lawyer.
>
> The common and sensible middle path: **mediate the substance, then pay an
> attorney for a one-time review of the draft before signing.** One consult, no
> retainer, a few hundred dollars. You keep mediation's cost and control and still
> get someone in your corner reading the document.
>
> Note also that a signed mediated agreement is generally an enforceable contract
> and hard to unwind — which is exactly why the review happens *before* the
> signature, not after.
>
> *Certified mediator in Florida. Which is why this answer is information about
> the role and not advice about your situation.*

## 6. "What is a simplified dissolution of marriage in Florida?"

**Narrow, factual, Florida-specific. Low competition, and precisely the kind of definitional question AI engines answer by quoting one source.**

> **Short answer: it's Florida's streamlined divorce process — roughly $500–$800 and about 30 days — available only to couples who meet a strict set of conditions.**
>
> ### Eligibility — you must meet all of these
>
> 1. One spouse has **resided in Florida for 6 months** before filing
>    (Fla. Stat. § 61.021)
> 2. **No minor or dependent children** of the marriage, and the wife is not
>    pregnant
> 3. **Neither spouse is seeking alimony**
> 4. You **agree completely** on the division of property and debts
> 5. Both agree the marriage is irretrievably broken
> 6. **Both are willing to appear** at the final hearing
>
> Fail any one of these and you're on the regular uncontested track instead. The
> children condition is the one that disqualifies most people, and there's no
> workaround for it.
>
> ### What it costs and how long it takes
>
> - Filing fee: **~$408**, plus **$10** for issuance of summons (slight county
>   variation)
> - Clerk's forms packet: often around **$14**
> - **Total: roughly $500–$800**
> - **Timeline: about 30 days** from filing to final judgment
>
> Florida has **no mandatory waiting period** of the kind some states impose,
> which is why the timeline is this short.
>
> ### How to actually do it
>
> 1. Get the Simplified Dissolution packet from your county clerk.
> 2. Complete the petition and both financial affidavits. Both spouses sign, and
>    signatures must be witnessed as the forms require.
> 3. File with the clerk in your county and pay the fee.
> 4. Attend the final hearing together. It is typically brief — the judge confirms
>    you meet the criteria and that the agreement is yours.
>
> Many clerks operate a **self-help center** that reviews your forms for
> completeness. They can't give legal advice, but catching a defective form before
> filing is most of what a pro se filer actually needs.
>
> Can't afford the fee? File an **Application for Determination of Civil Indigent
> Status** — approval waives the filing fee, but not service of process or
> mediation costs.
>
> ### Do you need a mediator for this?
>
> Honestly, usually not. Simplified dissolution *requires* that you already agree
> on everything — and if you do, there's nothing to mediate. Buy the packet and
> file.
>
> Where mediation helps is one step earlier: you want the simplified route, you're
> close to agreement, and one or two items are blocking you. A single session to
> resolve those can qualify you for a $600 divorce instead of a $15,000 one. That's
> the case where paying a mediator is obviously worth it — and it's a narrower case
> than most mediators will admit.
>
> *Certified mediator in Florida, working mostly with self-represented couples.
> Process information, not legal advice.*
